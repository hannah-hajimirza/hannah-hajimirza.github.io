De-risking One Risk, Concentrating Another: Evidence from Carbon Pricing in Syndicated Bank Lending
Working paper · 2025

Carbon pricing changes the relative attractiveness of carbon-intensive lending opportunities for many banks at once. If banks respond by reallocating toward the same remaining sectors, individual de-risking can increase common exposures across the banking system. I test this possibility using the staggered introduction of carbon pricing across sectors and jurisdictions and global syndicated lending. Banks with greater preexisting exposure to newly priced activities reduce carbon-intensive lending and become less diversified. The decline in diversification is not confined to the sectors directly affected by carbon pricing: among the sectors in which banks continue to lend, credit becomes more unevenly distributed. Yet banks do not converge toward a common replacement portfolio. Cross-bank portfolio overlap falls across industry, climate-policy, and geographic classifications; exposed banks become no more similar to one another than to less exposed banks, and they do not disproportionately reallocate toward sectors already common in bank portfolios. Carbon pricing therefore generates concentration without commonality: individual bank portfolios become narrower, but the banking system does not become more homogeneous through the asset-commonality channel.

Biodiversity risk and bank credit allocation: Sustainability commitments and double materiality
With Hossein Asgharian, Sara Jonsson, and Lu Liu · SSRN · November 2025

This paper examines how sustainability commitments shape banks’ incorporation of biodiversity risks into credit allocation. Using global syndicated loan data matched with firm-level measures of biodiversity impact and dependency, we find that all lenders extend smaller loans to more biodiversity-dependent firms, consistent with dependency risk being treated as financially material. By contrast, only UNEP FI–committed lenders reduce credit to high-impact borrowers, and only after joining, ruling out pre-existing greener preferences. This pattern is consistent with a double materiality perspective, whereby committed lenders respond not only to borrowers’ dependence on nature but also to their adverse impacts on biodiversity. The response strengthens over time and extends from GHG-related to non-GHG biodiversity impacts. Biodiversity risks have limited influence on secondary-market loan sales, except for modest evidence that committed syndicates sell loans to borrowers with high GHG-related biodiversity impacts. Overall, commitments mainly affect credit allocation at origination.
